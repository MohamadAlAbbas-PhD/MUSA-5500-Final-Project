Step 1 · From children to clusters

A DHS cluster is a village or urban neighbourhood where about 25–30 households were interviewed. For each cluster I calculate:

  • Anemia (%): the share of tested children aged 6–59 months with hemoglobin below 11 g/dL (adjusted for altitude by DHS)
  • Open defecation (%): the share of households with no toilet facility
  • Wealth: the average household wealth quintile (1 = poorest fifth of the country, 5 = richest)
  • Mothers with secondary school (%), urban/rural, and the interview month

Step 2 · Raster analysis: malaria and rainfall around each cluster

DHS moves every cluster’s GPS point by up to 2 km (urban) or 5 km (rural) to protect privacy. So instead of reading the raster at the point, I draw a buffer of that size around each point (in an equal-area projection, so the distances are in true metres) and take the mean raster value inside the buffer with rasterstats.zonal_stats.

  • Malaria: the Malaria Atlas Project raster for the year of the interview, read with rasterio.
  • Rainfall anomaly: with xarray, I sum CHIRPS rainfall over the 3 months before the interview month. I compare that total to the same 3 calendar months in every year from 1981 to 2010 at the same pixel: z = (this year − 30-year average) / 30-year standard deviation. Negative means drier than normal, positive means wetter.
  • Typical annual rainfall: the 1981–2010 average.

As a check, the rainfall anomalies correlate at 0.78 with the ones computed independently in R from the daily CHIRPS v3 data in my other project. The two use different CHIRPS versions and different window definitions, so they should not match exactly.

Step 3 · Clustering: k-means profiles and DBSCAN hotspots

  • k-means (scikit-learn) on the three standardized measures. Choosing k: the elbow plot and the silhouette score both point to k = 5.
  • DBSCAN on the latitude/longitude of the “all three high” clusters, with haversine (great-circle) distance. Settings: radius 50 km, at least 15 clusters. A sensitivity check covers radii of 25–100 km.

Step 4 · Machine learning: predicting anemia

  • Train/test split of 80/20, 5-fold cross-validation, and a grid search over random forest depth, leaf size, and features per split.
  • A linear regression pipeline (StandardScaler → LinearRegression) as the baseline.
  • Spatial lag: the average anemia of the 10 nearest training clusters, found with a BallTree and haversine distance. Test clusters only “see” training neighbours, so no answers leak in.
  • Permutation importance shows which features matter. Out-of-fold predictions give every cluster a prediction from a model that never saw it, which feeds the error map.

Step 5 · Privacy-safe aggregation and the website

DHS rules do not allow publishing cluster GPS points or microdata. The raw DHS data and every cluster-level file stay on my computer. They are excluded by .gitignore and never pushed to GitHub. Everything on this site is aggregated:

  • Hexagons (H3 resolution 4, ~23 km edge, ~1,770 km²), shown only if they hold at least 3 clusters. The real H3 cell ids are dropped.
  • Admin-1 regions, shown only with at least 5 clusters.
  • Malaria and rainfall rasters are open data and are shown in full.

📓 Notebook 6 · Privacy-safe exports shows the full code and outputs.

Visualization tools: a hand-built story website (HTML, CSS and JavaScript with Leaflet, reading the exported files, as in the Week 12 lab), plus folium for the Leaflet maps (layer control, a custom measure picker, synced dual maps, raster image overlays) and Altair for the linked charts (brushing, click-to-filter, legend selection, sliders, drop-downs). The site is built with Quarto and hosted on GitHub Pages.

python website/sync_assets.py    # copy the public exports into the story website
quarto render                    # notebooks are rendered with their saved outputs

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional data: this is about association and prediction, not cause and effect.
  • Surveys from different years are pooled (2000–2021). A hexagon can mix surveys taken 15 years apart.
  • Malaria is modelled, not measured in the cluster. MAP surfaces are themselves smoothed predictions.
  • Small samples: many clusters have 5–15 tested children, so cluster anemia rates are noisy. This limits how well any model can do.
  • GPS displacement blurs the raster extraction, which the buffers only partly fix.
  • Country coverage: only surveys that tested children’s hemoglobin and released GPS are included. In this extract, DR Congo and Côte d’Ivoire have no GPS, Zambia and Chad have no anemia classification, and Kenya has too few tested children with complete data. All five drop out.
